Xigaze Earthquake

On January 7, 2025, a magnitude 6.8 earthquake struck Tingri County in Xigaze, Tibet, with a focal depth of 10 kilometres. Following the disaster, I traveled from Chengdu to team up with a non-governmental foundation in Lhasa, before driving out to the frontline to document the affected townships in Tingri.

It was a grueling challenge, both physically and emotionally. Navigating through Tingri—where the altitude averages well over 4,300 metres—involved endless hours of driving along treacherous roads, followed by trekking through rubble with a heavy camera pack. At this extreme altitude, every breath felt earned, and the weight on my back grew heavier with every step.

I stepped inside numerous homes collapsed by the tremors, trying my best to connect with the affected Tibetan villagers. Because most of them spoke no Mandarin, our communication transcended words; I had to read their stories through the meager belongings they managed to salvage from the dangerous ruins, and the deep, silent lines of worry etched across their faces.

Yet, in this land deeply saturated by Tibetan Buddhism, I noticed something remarkable. The villagers did not exhibit the overwhelming helplessness often witnessed in the wake of such disasters. Instead, their enduring faith served as a fortress of strength amid the ruins. Their devotion was raw and intensely genuine. Amid the shattered concrete, one striking scene caught my eye: while secular portraits required by external authority lay buried or discarded in the rubble, the villagers followed a collective instinct to carefully dig out the sacred icon of Düsum Khyenpa (the founder of the Karma Kagyu sect). They wiped the dust clean from his face and reverently placed his portrait by the roadside.

Journeys in Tibet are always complex yet deeply fascinating. As I was leaving one village to head towards the next township, a young Tibetan man flagged me down on the roadside, asking for a lift. Driving down those desolate, unfamiliar plateau roads, his companionship was a welcome blessing.

As it turned out, he was a university student studying in Lhasa. When the earthquake hit, he threw caution to the wind and rushed back to Tingri. Only after seeing with his own eyes that his family was safe was he now heading back to his studies. During our drive, he shared a fascinating perspective rooted in the Buddhist concept of Karma. He was utterly convinced that the earthquake happened because human over-development had disrupted the natural environment, prompting Avalokiteshvara (the Bodhisattva of Compassion) to shake the earth as a stern warning for humanity to live in harmony with nature. He spoke of the deity with such absolute certainty, as if the Bodhisattva were a close, personal friend.

In the aftermath of the tragedy, state institutions and private charities have poured immense manpower and resources into the relief and rebuilding efforts. I have no doubt that in the near future, stronger and more beautiful homes will be built for them, and their daily rhythms will return to normal.

Yet, standing among the dust of these ruins, a haunting question lingers in my heart: as the physical houses are successfully rebuilt, who will come to care for their souls?
